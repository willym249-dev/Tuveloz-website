# Maryland changes the plan — read before using anything else

Five findings. One expands the business, one constrains it, one is a lever that exists in no other state.

---

## 1. Free care under 200% FPL is the law here, not hospital discretion

Nationally, charity care is whatever each hospital decides — published limits run anywhere from 41% to 600% of the federal poverty level, so two hospitals in one city answer the same family differently.

**Maryland is not like that.** State law requires hospitals to provide **free medically necessary care to patients at or below 200% of the federal poverty level**, and **reduced-cost care below 500% FPL where there is financial hardship**.

Two consequences:

- **The screen catches far more people than I told you earlier.** Under the agreement, everything written off through assistance is free to the client. In Maryland that is a bigger share of cases. It shrinks the fee base and makes the trust position much stronger — you are the person who tells Marylanders about a statutory entitlement most of them do not know they have.
- **The 240-day window is the part nobody uses.** Income is measured at the time of service *or* updated for a change in the patient's financial circumstances occurring **within 240 days after the first bill**. So somebody who lost a job, got sick, or had hours cut after the bill arrived can still qualify. Ask every client the question directly: *"Has anything changed with your income since this bill showed up?"* Nobody asks it.

Also in force since **1 October 2025** (HB 428, HB 1020, HB 268), and worth saying out loud to a frightened client on the first call:

- Providers, their agents, and debt collectors **may not report medical debt to a credit bureau**, and credit agencies may not carry it.
- **No interest** may be charged to a patient who qualifies for free or reduced-cost care. Check every bill for it.
- **No money judgment lien on a primary residence** for medical debt.
- The reporting prohibition is understood to attach to provider–collection agency contracts **entered into on or after 1 October 2025**; a contract lacking the required term is reported to be unenforceable. **This does not void the underlying debt**, and the date gate has not been verified from enacted text here — confirm both before raising it.

## 2. The Debt Settlement Services Act is a direct hit — restructure around it

Maryland's Debt Settlement Services Act (Financial Institutions, Title 12, Subtitle 10) requires **registration with the Commissioner of Financial Regulation through NMLS** for anyone providing debt settlement services to a Maryland consumer.

The definition is broad enough to reach us: any service that **"negotiates, settles, reduces, or in any way alters the terms"** of a debt between a consumer and an unsecured creditor or debt collector. **A medical bill is unsecured debt.** Calling a hospital and asking them to take $4,000 on an $8,000 balance is squarely inside that sentence.

The fee rules under § 12-1010 also bite: no fee for a consultation, no fee for pulling a credit report, and a services fee only after the agreement is signed, at least one debt has actually been renegotiated, **and the consumer has made at least one payment**.

**On the $50,000 surety bond: unresolved.** One source ties it to holding client money, another presents it as a flat registration requirement. This kit never holds client money, so the difference matters — and the enacted text has not been checked. **Do not state a bond figure as a registration cost.** See the correction in the claims register.

### The structure adopted while classification is unresolved — not a theory that it sits outside the Act

The reasoning below is **why the risk looks lower**, not a conclusion that any of it sits outside the Act. **No service here is classified.** An informal response from OFR would be useful and is still not a binding safe harbour:

| Service | Why it is not debt settlement |
| --- | --- |
| **Billing error correction** | You are disputing that the amount claimed is *correct*. That is a challenge to validity, not a negotiation of terms. |
| **Financial assistance advocacy** | You are helping someone claim a statutory entitlement from a hospital. No creditor negotiation occurs. |
| **Insurance appeals** | That is between the patient and their health plan under federal appeal rules. Not a creditor at all. |
| ~~Balance negotiation~~ | **This one is debt settlement.** Asking a creditor to accept less than the balance is the exact conduct the Act describes. |

**So: launch with the first three, free, with the consumer signing and sending — and leave balance negotiation out entirely.** This reduces exposure while the question is open. It does not resolve it, and it is not a finding that these activities sit outside the Act. That is not a big sacrifice in Maryland — with statutory free care under 200% FPL and reduced-cost care to 500%, the assistance route reaches most of the people negotiation would have reached, and it reaches them by right rather than by favor.

Balance negotiation is out of scope entirely, and the contingency-fee agreement that once contemplated it is quarantined outside this kit.

### What could not be verified, and what to ask the lawyer

The statute sites were unreachable from here, so **the exact percentage fee cap and the full exemption list are unconfirmed.** A "15% of debt handled" figure appears in coverage of a *proposed* bill — that is a report about a proposal, not confirmed law, and it is not being treated as fact.

Ask counsel exactly these, in this order:

1. Does billing-error dispute work, financial assistance advocacy, and insurance appeal representation fall outside "debt settlement services" as defined in § 12-1001?
2. If we ever add balance negotiation, what does registration require and what is the actual fee cap in § 12-1010?
3. Are there exemptions we fall inside already?
4. Does anything else — collection agency licensing, the Maryland Consumer Protection Act — reach this free, non-representative pilot? And separately, would it reach a fee-charging or representative model, if one were ever proposed?

That is a tight, cheap list because the questions are specific.

## 3. HSCRC — the lever that exists nowhere else

Maryland is the **only state in the country** where hospital rates are set by a regulator. Since 1971 the Health Services Cost Review Commission has held rate-setting authority over every general acute hospital in the state, for all payers, with rates updated on the commission's own schedule — check the actual rate order and its effective date.

Be careful about how far that goes. Recent changes mean Maryland hospitals now bill different amounts across payer categories under separate global budgets, and the system caps what hospitals may charge **in aggregate** while letting them vary amounts between patients. So "everyone in Maryland pays the identical price" is too strong a claim and should never be said to a billing office.

What is true and usable:

- Charges sit inside a **regulated rate structure**, not a price a hospital invented. **Work from the actual rate order and its effective date**, for the specific hospital, rate year, revenue centre, and unit — and separate facility charges from physician, ambulance, laboratory, dental, and pharmacy bills first, because those do not follow the same rules.
- There is a **state commission with authority over hospital rates** — which gives you an escalation rung no advocate in the other 49 states has.

**Add it to the ladder above the compliance line, and use it as a question rather than a threat:** *"Can you show me how this charge reconciles to the hospital's HSCRC-approved rate structure?"* A Maryland billing office knows exactly what that means. Asking it politely, with an account number, changes the temperature of a call.

## 4. Facility fees leave a paper trail here

The **Facility Fee Right-to-Know Act** (SB632, effective 1 July 2021) requires a hospital to disclose outpatient facility fees **at the time the appointment is scheduled** — orally and in writing — and to obtain a **signed patient acknowledgment**.

That turns a vague complaint into a documentary question. Not *"why is there a facility fee"* but:

> "Can you produce the signed acknowledgment for this appointment?"

Ask the facility to produce its notice and acknowledgment record. **A document the patient does not have proves nothing** — they may simply not have kept it, and the notice rules carry scope limits and exceptions that have not been checked from here. If it stays unresolved, that is a question for HSCRC or HEAU, not a conclusion to assert. Add the request to the audit on every outpatient or hospital-clinic visit.

## 5. HEAU is the best escalation in the country, and it is free

Maryland's Attorney General runs the **Health Education and Advocacy Unit**, which mediates medical billing and insurance disputes at no cost — covering private insurance, medical billing, **hospital financial assistance applications**, medical debt collection, and surprise out-of-network emergency bills.

HEAU publishes its own outcome figures on its site. **Quote them from there if you quote them at all, and never imply that anything we prepare is why a case succeeds** — we have no evidence of that.

Two consequences, and they pull in opposite directions, which is fine:

- **It is free, so you tell clients about it** — the same rule as Dollar For. Never let a client discover later that a free state service existed and you did not mention it.


**There is no pricing conversation yet.** The service runs as a free pilot until Maryland classification is settled in writing. The scope decision stands on the unresolved classification and on § 12-1010's payment-first fee bar, which are stronger grounds anyway.
