# Legal landscape — reference facts

Statutes, tests, thresholds, and penalties that apply to a vehicle-service
marketplace operating in Montgomery County, Maryland. Each entry states what
triggers the law, how it operates mechanically, who enforces it, and what the
exposure is.

This file records facts, not recommendations. Sources are linked so every
figure can be checked. Rules change — verify anything before relying on it.

Last verified: August 2026.

## 1. Worker classification

### Maryland: the ABC test

Maryland's unemployment insurance law (Md. Code, Labor & Employment § 8-205)
uses an **ABC test**. A worker is **presumed to be an employee**, and the
business carries the burden of proving otherwise.

All three prongs must be satisfied:

- **(A)** The individual is free from direction and control over performance of
  the work.
- **(B)** The individual is customarily engaged in an independent business or
  occupation of the same nature as the work performed.
- **(C)** The work is outside the usual course of business of the person it is
  performed for, **or** the work is performed outside any place of business of
  that person.

Mechanically, prong C is disjunctive in Maryland — either branch satisfies it.
That differs from California's version, where the equivalent prong has no "or"
escape. Failing any single prong makes the worker an employee for unemployment
insurance purposes regardless of what a contract says.

The **Workplace Fraud Act** (Labor & Employment § 3-901 et seq.), which adds
separate penalties and recordkeeping duties, applies **only to the construction
and landscaping industries**. Vehicle services are outside its scope.

Source: [Maryland Department of Labor — Worker Classification Protection](https://labor.maryland.gov/workplace/wfempfaqs.shtml),
[Are You Complying with the Workplace Fraud Act?](https://labor.maryland.gov/workplace/wcpcomplying.shtml)

### Federal: two separate tests

- **FLSA** (wage and hour) uses an *economic realities* multifactor test asking
  whether the worker is economically dependent on the business or in business
  for themselves. Which version of that test applies currently depends on **who
  is asking** — see the timeline below.
- **IRS** uses a *common-law control* test grouped into behavioral control,
  financial control, and type of relationship. A business or worker can request
  an IRS determination on Form SS-8. Section 530 of the Revenue Act of 1978
  provides safe-harbor relief from employment tax liability where a business
  had a reasonable basis for treating workers as contractors, filed all
  required 1099s, and treated similar workers consistently.

### FLSA rule status — the split between enforcement and litigation

The operative test currently differs depending on who is applying it. Verified
August 2026:

| Date | Event |
|---|---|
| Jan 2024 | DOL 2024 final rule takes effect — six-factor totality-of-the-circumstances analysis, no factor predetermined to carry more weight. |
| — | Five lawsuits filed challenging the rule. **All five stayed**, on the Department's representation that it intended to reconsider the rule. |
| **May 1, 2025** | DOL issues **Field Assistance Bulletin 2025-1**, instructing Wage and Hour Division field staff to **stop applying** the 2024 rule in investigations. |
| — | For enforcement, DOL reverts to **Fact Sheet #13 (July 2008)** and **Opinion Letter FLSA2019-6**. Applies to any matter where back wages or civil money penalties were unpaid as of May 1, 2025. |
| **Feb 26–27, 2026** | DOL announces and publishes an **NPRM (RIN 1235-AA46)** to rescind and replace the 2024 rule. Proposed framework restores the **2021 rule's** test: two core factors — **control over the work** and **opportunity for profit or loss** — carrying greater weight and generally determinative. |
| **Apr 28, 2026** | 60-day comment period closes. |
| As of Aug 2026 | **No final rule located.** Searches surface only the NPRM and commentary on it. This is an absence of evidence, not confirmation that no final rule issued — check the [DOL rulemaking page](https://www.dol.gov/agencies/whd/flsa/misclassification/2026rulemaking) directly. |

**The operative split:**

- **DOL investigations** — 2008 Fact Sheet #13 economic-realities framework.
- **Private FLSA lawsuits** — the **2024 rule remains in effect**. It is still
  on the books until a final rule replaces it, and a private plaintiff can
  invoke it. Ceasing enforcement did not repeal it.

That distinction is the practical one: a worker suing directly is not bound by
the DOL's enforcement posture.

### How exposure accrues

Misclassification liability is **per worker, per period**, and stacks across
agencies: unpaid unemployment insurance contributions, workers' compensation
premiums, unpaid payroll taxes with interest and penalties, and — under FLSA —
unpaid minimum wage and overtime with liquidated damages that can double the
back-pay amount. A single determination for one worker generally establishes
the classification for every similarly situated worker.

### Settlements on record

- Uber, New Jersey unemployment-tax assessment: **$100 million** (2022).
- Uber, Massachusetts and Lyft, Massachusetts, combined settlements with the
  state Attorney General: **$175 million** (2024).
- *Vazquez v. Jan-Pro Franchising Int'l*: franchise "owners" held to be
  employees under the ABC test.
- *Awuah v. Coverall North America*: franchisee cleaners held to be employees
  under Massachusetts law.

## 2. Telephone Consumer Protection Act (TCPA) — texts and calls

**Trigger:** sending a *marketing* call or text to a mobile number.
Transactional contact about a transaction the recipient initiated is treated
differently and does not require marketing consent.

**Requirement:** prior express written consent, which must
- clearly disclose the nature of the messages,
- identify the seller,
- state that consent is not a condition of purchase, and
- be an affirmative act — a pre-checked box does not qualify.

**Revocation:** since **April 11, 2025**, consumers may revoke by any
reasonable method — reply keyword, email, phone, web form, or in person.
Businesses must honor a revocation within **10 business days**. One
non-marketing confirmation message is permitted.

**Damages:** **$500 per message**, trebled to **$1,500 per message** for
willful or knowing violations. There is no cap, and it is a private right of
action, which is why the class-action bar is active in this area.

**Regulatory note:** the FCC's "one-to-one consent" rule was **vacated by the
Eleventh Circuit in January 2025**, so the broader consent framework described
above remains what applies.

**Case on record:** *In re Jiffy Lube International Text Spam Litigation* —
franchisee Heartland Automotive settled for **$47 million** ($35M cash value)
after sending one 45%-off oil-change text to **2.3 million** recipients. The
numbers had been supplied by customers on their own service invoices. Class
members received roughly $17 in service credit; plaintiffs' counsel received
$4.75 million.

Sources: [Burr & Forman on the Jiffy Lube settlement](https://www.burr.com/telephone-consumer-protection-act/the-tcpa-in-re-jiffy-lube-internationa-35252/),
[BCLP on the April 2025 revocation rules](https://www.bclplaw.com/en-US/events-insights-news/the-tcpas-new-opt-out-rules-take-effect-on-april-11-2025-what-does-this-mean-for-businesses.html),

## 3. CAN-SPAM — commercial email

**Trigger:** email whose primary purpose is commercial advertising.

**Requirements:** accurate header and "from" information, a non-deceptive
subject line, identification as an advertisement, a **valid physical postal
address**, a working opt-out mechanism, and honoring opt-outs within **10
business days**.

**Damages:** civil penalties up to roughly **$53,000 per email** (the figure is
inflation-adjusted annually, so confirm the current amount). Enforcement is by
the FTC and state attorneys general. **There is no private right of action** —
this is the structural difference from the TCPA, and it is why email exposure
behaves very differently from text exposure at the same volume.

## 4. Maryland Automotive Repair Facilities Act

Md. Code, Commercial Law, **Title 14, Subtitle 10** (§ 14-1001 et seq.).

**Trigger:** performing automotive repair work for a consumer in Maryland.

**Mechanics:**

- A repair facility must **offer a written estimate** when the repair will
  exceed **$50**.
- Repairs may not begin without customer approval of the estimate. Approval may
  be written or oral.
- A customer may **waive** the estimate in writing, if the waiver is clear and
  voluntary.
- Where a written estimate was given, the facility **may not charge more than
  110% of it** — a 10% ceiling over the estimate.
- The authorization form must state the customer's rights **immediately before
  the signature space**, in easily readable type, physically separated from
  other terms, under the heading **"Customer's Rights."**
- Oral notice of rights is permitted where the vehicle was towed in, or left
  when the facility was closed.

Enforcement runs through the Maryland Consumer Protection Division; a violation
is also an unfair or deceptive trade practice under Title 13.

Sources: [Md. Commercial Law Title 14, Subtitle 10 (Justia, 2024)](https://law.justia.com/codes/maryland/commercial-law/title-14/subtitle-10/),
[§ 14-1008 statute text](https://mgaleg.maryland.gov/mgawebsite/laws/StatuteText?article=gcl&section=14-1008)

## 5. Maryland Consumer Protection Act

Md. Code, Commercial Law, **Title 13** (§ 13-301 defines unfair, abusive, or
deceptive trade practices).

**Trigger:** any statement or omission in consumer-facing marketing, terms, or
sales that is false, misleading, or omits a material fact.

**Mechanics:** the Consumer Protection Division of the Maryland Attorney
General's office investigates and may seek injunctions, restitution, and civil
penalties. Title 13 also carries a **private right of action** for actual
damages, and attorney's fees may be awarded.

Categories that recur in enforcement against marketplaces: undisclosed or
drip-priced fees, claims of verification or screening that was not performed,
and representations about service availability that are not accurate.

## 6. FTC Act § 5 — deception and unfairness (federal)

**Trigger:** a representation, omission, or practice likely to mislead a
reasonable consumer, material to their decision.

Enforcement is by the FTC, by administrative action or federal suit; remedies
include injunctions, redress, and civil penalties. No private right of action.

**Actions on record involving marketplace platforms:**

- **HomeAdvisor / Angi**, 2023: **$7.2 million** to settle FTC charges of
  misleading service providers about the quality and source of leads sold to
  them.
- **Uber**, 2017: **$20 million** to settle FTC charges of overstating driver
  earnings and vehicle-financing terms.
- **Uber**, 2016: **$28.5 million** class settlement over "industry-leading
  background checks" and "safe rides fee" claims.

The pattern in all three is claims made to the *supply* side or about safety
screening, not claims about price.

## 7. Maryland Online Data Privacy Act (MODPA)

**Status:** in effect **October 1, 2025**. Enforcement began **April 1, 2026**.
A 60-day cure period runs until **April 1, 2027**, after which cure is
discretionary and the Attorney General may act immediately.

**Applicability thresholds** — a business is covered if, in a calendar year, it
either:

- processes personal data of **35,000 or more** Maryland consumers (excluding
  data processed solely to complete a payment transaction), **or**
- processes personal data of **10,000 or more** consumers **and** derives 20%
  or more of gross revenue from selling personal data.

The 35,000 threshold is materially lower than the 100,000 used by most state
privacy laws.

**Requirements once covered:** privacy notice, processor contracts, data
security obligations, data protection assessments for high-risk processing, and
consumer rights (access, deletion, correction, portability, opt-out).

**Sensitive data:** collection, processing, or sharing is permitted only when
**strictly necessary** to provide a product or service the consumer requested.
**Sale of sensitive data is prohibited outright**, and consent does not cure
that prohibition. This is stricter than most state analogues, where consent
generally suffices.

Sources: [OneTrust on MODPA](https://www.onetrust.com/blog/marylands-online-data-privacy-act-modpa-key-rules-and-requirements/),
[Verified Credentials on the April 2026 enforcement date](https://news.verifiedcredentials.com/maryland-online-data-privacy-act-enforcement-begins-on-april-1-2026)

## 8. Form 1099-K — payment reporting

**Trigger:** acting as, or paying through, a third-party settlement
organization (TPSO).

**Current threshold:** a TPSO must file Form 1099-K where gross reportable
payments to a payee exceed **$20,000** *and* the number of transactions exceeds
**200**. Both conditions must be met.

The **One Big Beautiful Bill Act**, signed **July 4, 2025**, repealed the
American Rescue Plan Act's $600 threshold and retroactively restored the
$20,000 / 200-transaction rule.

**Exception:** payment *card* transactions are reportable with **no minimum
threshold**. The $20,000/200 rule applies only to third-party network
transactions.

Separately, Form **1099-NEC** reporting for nonemployee compensation operates
on its own threshold and is not affected by the 1099-K rules.

Sources: [IRS Form 1099-K FAQs](https://www.irs.gov/newsroom/form-1099-k-faqs),
[RSM US on the OBBBA thresholds](https://rsmus.com/insights/services/business-tax/irs-updates-obbba-new-reporting-thresholds.html)

## 9. Arbitration clauses and class waivers

**Mechanics:** the Federal Arbitration Act makes arbitration agreements
enforceable. *Epic Systems Corp. v. Lewis* (Supreme Court, 2018) held that
agreements requiring individualized arbitration, with class and collective
proceedings waived, are enforceable and are not barred by the National Labor
Relations Act.

**Effect:** a class waiver converts one potential class action into individual
proceedings. The companies in this sector that faced classwide judgments —
*Dynamex*, *Jan-Pro* — were litigating without one; the companies that faced
individual arbitrations instead had them.

**Limits worth knowing:** the *Ending Forced Arbitration of Sexual Assault and
Sexual Harassment Act* (2022) makes such clauses unenforceable at the
claimant's election for those specific claims. Mass-arbitration filings have
also emerged as a counter-strategy, where large numbers of individual demands
are filed at once and per-case arbitration fees fall on the business.

## 10. Section 230 — what it does and does not cover

47 U.S.C. § 230 bars treating an interactive computer service as the publisher
or speaker of information provided by another content provider.

**Covers:** third-party content — provider-written profile text, customer
reviews, listing descriptions.

**Does not cover:** a platform's **own** representations, its own conduct, or
claims framed as the platform's negligence in how it designed or operated the
service. Courts have allowed claims to proceed against platforms on theories of
negligent undertaking and product design where the claim did not depend on
third-party speech. Section 230 also does not bar federal criminal law or
intellectual-property claims.

Practically, it protects what a provider writes on their profile. It does not
protect a statement Tuveloz makes about that provider.
